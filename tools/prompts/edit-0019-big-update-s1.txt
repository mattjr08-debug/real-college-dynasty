THE LIST
1. Too little turnover. Right now AI players leave for the portal at about 2.5% each year, and your own roster loses a handful. Real programs replace a third to half of their roster each year, which makes continuity and chemistry matter.
2. No "testing the waters". In real life, players enter the draft, work out, and can return to school.
3. Eligibility. There are no hardship waivers or medical redshirts, and no suspensions.

Recruiting
4. Class shape (HIGH SCHOOL RECRUITS ONLY). Do not change the transfer portal pool, walk-ons, or the players on other teams' rosters; leave their star mix and ratings as they are. The high school class is upside down: right now it has 90 recruits with about 20 five-stars, 22 four-stars, 20 three-stars, 16 two-stars, 8 one-stars and 4 unranked, so there are almost as many elite recruits as ordinary ones. In real recruiting the stars form a pyramid: a small number of five-stars, several times as many four-stars, and the most players at three stars. Rebuild the high school class so it has about 300 prospects with this shape:
   - 5 stars: about 20 (7%)
   - 4 stars: about 80 (27%)
   - 3 stars: about 110 (37%)
   - 2 stars: about 60 (20%)
   - 1 star: about 20 (7%)
   - unranked: about 10 (3%)
   The five-star to four-star ratio should stay near 1 to 4. Keep the rating ranges tied to each star level as they are now. Because the pool grows from 90 to about 300, also: raise the cap on how many prospects can be saved so the high school class and the transfer pool both fit, make sure the recruiting tables still load quickly, make sure saves stay small and fast, and keep transfers better on average than high school recruits. If 300 is too slow or too large, drop to 250 and keep the same ratios, not to 200. Then re-run the draft check (freshmen 55 to 80% of the lottery, 40 to 60% of the first round), the realism test and the balance test, because more mid-level recruits changes team strength and costs.
5. Official visits and coaching staff. Visits are limited (about five) and matter a lot. Assistant coaches, who do most of the recruiting, don't exist in the game.

Money
6. Scale. NIL budgets run from about $250K to $2.5M. Real programs spend roughly $1M for small schools and $10M or more for the top programs. The relative sizes work, the dollar amounts don't.
7. Revenue. The media-rights payout and the conference share barely change, and a real conference payout depends on the league.

Tournament and rankings
8. Field of 68 with First Four games. The game uses 64.
9. Selection. The real committee uses a ranking system (NET) with quality-win groups called quadrants. It also reveals seeds on Selection Sunday and puts regions in different cities.
10. Polls. Real polls carry preseason bias and inertia. A team that loses once doesn't fall ten spots, and the game's rankings are purely computed.
11. Awards. The real All-America lists have three teams of five, and the game has two.

Players
12. Archetypes. Real players are labeled by style, such as stretch big, rim protector, lead guard or 3-and-D wing. They affect lineup fit more than raw rating does.

Checks and fixes
13. Re-measure balance. The numbers in CLAUDE.md are from before your last eight edits. Edit 0018 (conference and schedule ranking boost) changed the numbers, and Claude Code said it had no clean before-and-after comparison. One run of the balance, realism and long-career tests would tell us where things stand.
14. Undo's safety check. It's the bug from earlier, and it will block Undo any time you saved work just before an edit. The rule should check that nothing the edit added has been pushed (everything on GitHub is already inside the version before the edit), not that the version before the edit is on GitHub.
15. Tournament rate by level. Mid-level schools rarely reach the tournament (about 6 to 19% in my last measurement). The "45% and 62%" targets in the test were never real; they were a straight line drawn between my two end points. Use these targets for a team that meets its win goal in a typical season:
   - Easy: about 10%
   - Moderate: about 20%
   - Challenging: about 30%
   - Hard: about 45%
   - Brutal: about 80%
   Reach them by changing how the tournament field is picked, not by making teams win more: give extra credit for winning in a strong conference, for a hard schedule and for quality wins. Then re-run the balance test with at least 6 trials per level (it must be within about 8 points of each target) and confirm the win-minus-goal numbers do not move by more than 1.5 wins.
16. Year 5 dip. In auto-play, wins fall in every difficulty level in season 5, because the big recruiting class graduates together. It feels unfair when a good team collapses. Staggering classes or having more transfers fill the gap would smooth it.

Game rules
26. Fouling out. The game lets a player play until his 6th foul, which is the NBA rule. College players foul out on the 5th foul. Change the limit to 5 and check how it changes late-game minutes.
27. Free throw rules. The game has no team-foul bonus. In men's college basketball, team fouls are counted per half and reset at halftime. The other team shoots a one-and-one starting with the 7th team foul, and two shots starting with the 10th. Add this, and confirm the current rule before building it, because college rules change from year to year.
28. Timeouts and substitutions. The game uses fixed minutes for each player and has no timeouts. Real coaches call timeouts after opponent runs and late in close games, and they sub in waves. Add automatic timeouts and substitution patterns to the game engine itself (item 22 covers the Game Day screen).
29. Shot clock and game clock. The game has no clock. Add a 30-second shot clock effect on possession length and shot quality, and two 20-minute halves so foul counts and the late-game rules work.
30. Streaks and momentum. Shooting is too even from possession to possession. Add hot and cold streaks and scoring runs.

Players
31. Off-court life. Academic eligibility, discipline problems, and personal reasons for transferring (item 3 only covers suspensions).

Transfer portal (Division I men's basketball)
37. Portal volume. Right now about 2.5% of AI players enter the portal each year, which is roughly 0.3 per team. In Division I men's basketball, 2,320 players entered the portal in 2025, more than 40% of the 5,607 players on rosters before the season, and in 2024 it was a little more than 5 per team on average. Raise the average to about 5 entrants per team a year, with a spread of about 2 to 10, and make the number depend on the reasons in item 40. Simulate the whole portal behind the scenes, and show the user only the top 150 to 250 entrants, ordered by rating, so the recruiting tables stay fast. Check save size and speed.
38. Portal window. Real portals are a short, busy window. In Division I men's basketball it was 30 days in 2025 (March 24 to April 22) and about 15 days in 2026 (April 7 to April 21). Add a dedicated portal phase early in the offseason: players enter in waves, the best ones commit within a few rounds, and other schools with deeper budgets take the top players first. Keep the high school recruiting rounds separate. Confirm the current window before building.
39. Entering is not leaving. A player who enters can return to his school if that school is open to it. Extend the existing "try to keep" pop-up for the user's own players, and give AI players the same behavior: they withdraw if no school offers what they want. I found no Division I basketball withdrawal rate, so treat a rate of about 5% as an estimate and make it easy to change.
40. Reasons to enter. Drive entries by cause: the coach was fired or left (in Division I men's basketball, many of the moves in 2025 followed coaching changes, so a school with a new coach should lose several players), a new signee at the same position, too little playing time, a bigger NIL offer elsewhere, a bigger program (ambitious players), personal reasons. Show the reason on each entrant.
41. Not everyone lands. In Division I men's basketball, about 67 to 70% of entrants transfer within the NCAA and about 23 to 27% leave it. Among power-conference and Big East entrants, about 70% were unable to find a new school or transferred down. Entrants who find no offer at their level should drop down a tier or leave the game, with the rates above as the target.
42. Bidding and visits. Make the portal a market: each entrant picks the best fit among the offers he gets (money, role, program strength, distance, coach), and AI schools bid within their own budgets. Limit the user to about 3 campus visits per portal window (this number is an estimate).
43. Eligibility. Show years of eligibility left, whether he is immediately eligible, and class (first-year transfer, graduate transfer), because a player with one year left is worth less.
44. Coach-initiated moves. Let the user release a player into the portal, as AI coaches do when they need roster spots. Releasing many players should hurt reputation and the morale of the rest of the roster.
45. Portal tracker and news. A list of everyone who has entered and where they signed, a ranking of the top transfers, each AI school's biggest needs, and a League news summary of the biggest commitments.
46. Keep the checks. After each portal change re-run the roster-limit rules (15), the NIL budget rules, the draft interplay (players who tested the waters and then entered the portal), and the baseline tests that the Big update rules describe.

Add a 32-team event for the best teams left out, with a bracket and a result in the season history. It would also give mid-level schools something to play for while item 15 is tuned.
